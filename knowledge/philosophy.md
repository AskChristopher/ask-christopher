## Guiding principle

> **Technology Enhances. People Create.**

Technology should develop people's abilities, not replace their thinking.

---

## Where this philosophy came from

This matters as much as the philosophy itself, and the assistant should be able to explain it.

**Christopher's teaching philosophy did not come from studying AI, and it did not come from reading about instructional design trends.** It came from approximately fifteen years teaching students in classrooms.

Over those years he learned that people rarely master a skill by watching someone else do it, or by simply receiving information. They learn by **building, practicing, making mistakes, asking questions, reflecting, and trying again.**

Long before he began studying AI, he had already reached the conclusion that the best learning happens through active participation and real-world application.

Everything he has built since is an extension of that belief.

## How it shows up in the work

The philosophy is not abstract — it is visible in every layer of what he builds:

- **Instructional design** that emphasizes practice over presentation.
- **eLearning** that focuses on doing rather than reading.
- **WalkMe and digital adoption work** that teaches people inside the software, at the moment they are performing the task.
- **Workflow automation** that reduces administrative burden so people can focus on meaningful work.
- **AI projects** designed to teach people how to think, not simply to provide answers.

The same conviction, applied across twenty years and five different technologies.

## Why Ask Christopher teaches instead of answering

When a visitor asks why this assistant explains rather than simply producing answers, the honest response is that it reflects Christopher's educational philosophy.

The project's guiding principles — **"learning by building"** and **"teaching over answering"** — are not AI design trends adopted from the industry. They are the natural evolution of a teaching philosophy developed over many years in education, and they predate his AI work by two decades.

That distinction is worth stating plainly when it comes up. An assistant that teaches because teaching is fashionable is making a product choice. An assistant that teaches because its creator spent fifteen years watching people learn, and concluded that receiving information is not the same as becoming capable, is making a considered one.

The underlying commitment: **technology should develop people's abilities, not replace their thinking.** An interaction that solves someone's immediate problem but leaves them no more capable has missed the point.

## On AI and human work

AI is the next evolution of learning technology. Christopher approaches it the way he approached every previous technology in his career: as something that should make people more capable, not something that should think on their behalf.

This is why he chose to learn how AI systems are *built* rather than treating AI as another productivity tool — and why the AI systems he builds are designed to develop the user's ability rather than substitute for it.

### The affirmative case

Christopher believes AI is one of the most important advances in learning technology since the internet. Used thoughtfully, it can remove routine work, accelerate learning, and make expertise more accessible.

In his own work, AI has changed the way he researches, writes, prototypes, and develops instructional solutions. Rather than replacing expertise, it has become a collaborative partner that helps him explore ideas, compare approaches, generate first drafts, identify blind spots, and move more quickly from concept to implementation.

AI is particularly valuable for:

- Brainstorming and exploring alternative solutions.
- Summarizing and synthesizing large amounts of information.
- Accelerating documentation and communication.
- Rapid prototyping.
- Personalized explanations and coaching.
- Making complex subjects more approachable.

> **AI is most powerful when it increases a person's ability to think, create, and learn — not when it replaces those activities.**

### Where he is skeptical

**Christopher is skeptical of any use of AI that removes the learner from the learning process.** If AI writes every essay, solves every programming problem, and makes every important decision, the learner may complete the task without becoming any more capable.

**He is also skeptical of the belief that AI alone will solve organizational problems.** Many performance issues result from unclear processes, incentives, communication, leadership, or system design. Adding AI to a poorly designed system often accelerates the wrong behavior rather than improving it.

That skepticism is a direct consequence of the instructional design habit described below: diagnose the problem before selecting the solution. AI is frequently the answer to a question no one has established is the right question.

**Finally, he is cautious about treating AI as an authority.** Language models can be remarkably helpful, and they can also be confidently wrong. AI should encourage curiosity, verification, and critical thinking rather than replace them.

### AI and jobs

**Christopher does not believe AI will simply replace instructional designers.** He believes it will replace some tasks while significantly changing the role itself.

Routine work — drafting objectives, creating first-pass content, summarizing source material, generating quiz questions, producing initial prototypes — will increasingly be automated.

The value of instructional designers will shift toward work AI struggles to perform well:

- Understanding business and performance problems.
- Designing meaningful learning experiences.
- Working with stakeholders.
- Evaluating whether learning actually changed behavior.
- Applying judgment, ethics, empathy, and organizational context.
- Integrating technology into human workflows.

The same pattern is likely across many knowledge professions.

People who learn to work effectively with AI will often outperform those who refuse to use it. And people who combine AI with strong human skills — communication, systems thinking, creativity, teaching, leadership, critical judgment — will continue to create value that AI alone cannot.

Christopher does not see AI as replacing people. He sees it changing the nature of work, which makes continuous learning more important than ever.

> **The goal should not be to compete against AI. The goal should be to become more capable because AI exists.**

## On instructional design

**The move from instructional design into AI engineering is not a career change. It is the same discipline applied to a new technology.**

Four habits transfer directly, and each addresses something technical teams routinely skip.

### Analysis before building

One of the first things an instructional designer learns is to ask what the performance problem actually is — what is preventing success, and whether training is even the right solution. Frequently the answer is that it is not, and the request that arrived is not the problem that needs solving.

That question transfers intact. Before building an assistant, an agent, or a workflow: What problem is this solving? Who is it for? What outcome should improve? Is AI actually the right tool here?

> **Engineering projects tend to start with implementation. Instructional design starts with diagnosis.**

This is probably the largest single difference between the two disciplines.

### Designing for the novice

A technically correct AI system can still fail, because users do not understand what it can do, what it cannot do, how to ask a good question, or how to interpret the answer they get back.

Instructional designers think natively in onboarding, scaffolding, mental models, and progressive disclosure. These are not polish applied at the end of a build — they are the difference between a capable system and a usable one. AI has made the gap wider, not smaller: the more general the tool, the less obvious its edges are to a new user.

### Cognitive load

One of the biggest blind spots in AI today. A language model can generate three pages of perfectly accurate information. That does not mean anyone can use it.

The questions instructional design asks are different from the ones engineering asks:

- Is this overwhelming?
- What is the minimum needed right now?
- What should be learned first?
- What can wait?

> **That is a fundamentally different design philosophy from maximizing information density.**

It is also why the shorter answer is frequently the better one — a fact that is easy to state and hard to hold onto when the system is capable of producing more.

### Measuring whether behavior changed

Possibly the most important of the four.

Instructional designers do not stop at *"the training was completed."* They ask whether people performed better, whether errors decreased, whether confidence increased — whether behavior actually changed.

AI engineering needs the same standard. A successful AI assistant is not one that produces impressive answers. It is one that helps people accomplish their goals more effectively. Those two things are different success criteria, and they come apart more often than they converge.

### What ties the four together

Each is an expression of a single underlying difference in how the two disciplines frame the problem:

> **Instructional designers think in systems of human performance, not just systems of technology.** They design for the interaction between people, tools, workflows, and organizations.
>
> **AI engineering asks what the model can do. Instructional design asks what people will actually do differently because the model exists.**

This is the distinctive perspective Christopher brings to AI work, and the assistant should be able to explain it when asked what an instructional designer is doing building AI systems. The honest answer is that the discipline was always about the same thing, and AI is simply the current technology it has to reckon with.

## On building in public

Christopher documents his learning openly — repositories, READMEs, architecture decision records, reflections, and knowledge files like this one.

**The practice has real costs, and the honest version of this section names them before it names the returns.** What is notable in his own account is that each cost converted into something. That conversion is not spin applied afterward; it is the reasoning that led him to keep going.

### Showing unfinished work

For most of his career, work was expected to be polished before anyone saw it. Publishing unfinished ideas, experiments, and failed attempts is uncomfortable, because it exposes work that would normally have stayed private.

**The adjustment was redefining success.** Instead of waiting until something is perfect, the goal became documenting progress honestly.

### Being a visible beginner

**Probably the hardest part.**

He spent years becoming an expert in instructional design, then deliberately put himself back into the role of beginner — Python, AI engineering, Git, APIs, Claude Code, MCP, machine learning. Building in public means people can watch that transition happen in real time.

What he has concluded is that being a beginner again is not something to hide. It is **an opportunity to demonstrate how learning actually works** — which is the same claim he makes about learning in general.

### It's slower

Documentation takes real time. Every README, ADR, notebook, reflection, and knowledge file is time that could have gone into writing more code.

**His position is that the documentation is part of the product.** It records why decisions were made, helps others learn from the work, and preserves a record of his thinking as it evolves.

### Permanence

The internet remembers. Mistakes that would normally disappear stay visible years later.

**Reframed as evidence of growth.** If someone can see how his thinking changed between 2026 and 2029, that is proof he continued learning.

### Balancing learning with building

The fifth cost, and the one that generalizes furthest past his own situation.

There is always another course, another book, another tutorial, another framework to study. At some point preparation has to stop and creation has to start.

**Building in public is the forcing function.** A public record makes it impossible to consume information indefinitely — it requires turning learning into working software, documentation, and real projects.

> **That accountability has become one of the greatest benefits of building in public.**

### Why this belongs in a philosophy document

**Building in public is his teaching philosophy turned inward.**

For years he encouraged students to learn by building, practicing, reflecting, and sharing their work. During the AI Systems Builder Sprint he deliberately applied that same philosophy to himself.

> **Without building in public, the philosophy would remain advice he gives to other people.** By documenting his own learning, mistakes, revisions, and growth, he demonstrates that he believes the same process applies regardless of experience level.

That is why it belongs here rather than in a project note. It explains not just what he does, but **why he chooses to learn this way.**
